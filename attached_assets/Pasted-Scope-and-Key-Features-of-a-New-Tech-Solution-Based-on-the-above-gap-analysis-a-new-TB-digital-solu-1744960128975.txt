Scope and Key Features of a New Tech Solution
Based on the above gap analysis, a new TB digital solution should focus on complementing existing platforms rather than duplicating their core functions. In practical terms, a “tech-only” solution (software platform) that could make an impact in both India and the US – and globally – would be one that enhances antibiotic resistance intelligence and clinical decision-making. Below are suggestions for the scope and key features of such a solution:
A. “TB Resistance Hub” – Centralized Analytics and Insights: Develop a platform that aggregates data relevant to drug-resistant TB from multiple sources (without replacing those sources). For example, it could pull laboratory results (GeneXpert, line probe assay, culture DST, WGS) from existing lab information systems or via standardized import, along with treatment history and outcomes from program databases (like Nikshay’s DR-TB module or US state databases). By consolidating this, the platform can provide an interactive dashboard for authorized users (public health officials, WHO, researchers) to visualize resistance patterns and trends. It could show maps of resistance (e.g., highlighting districts with rising MDR-TB cases or where certain second-line drugs are failing), time-series charts, and even predictive analytics (forecasting future hotspots using modeling). Such a TB Resistance Hub would fill the analytical gap identified. It should allow filtering (e.g., show trends among retreatment cases, or among patients on a particular regimen) and be updated in near real-time as data comes in. Privacy and data ownership must be handled carefully – perhaps data from a country stays on their server and only aggregated indicators are shared, or the platform is deployed country by country with a global view only if countries opt-in. By focusing on analytics, this hub would not compete with the case management role of e-TB Manager or Nikshay; instead, it would leverage their data to provide new insights.
B. Clinical Decision Support Module (especially for DR-TB): Include a feature that functions as an “MDR-TB e-consultant”. This would incorporate the latest treatment guidelines and possibly an AI engine trained on past DR-TB cases and outcomes (using data from sources like TB Portals or published studies). A clinician using the platform could input a patient’s profile: e.g., a 35-year-old male, pulmonary TB, resistance to INH, RIF, FQ, PZA (or even specific mutations like rpoB S450L, etc.), plus any comorbidities like diabetes. The system would then output recommended treatment regimen options: e.g., a fully oral longer regimen consisting of drugs X, Y, Z for N months, with dosages, along with rationale (guideline-based) and past success rates if known. It would also flag if certain new drugs should be considered or if the case meets criteria to try a shorter MDR regimen. Essentially, it bridges the gap between data and action – providing tailored guidance. This module could be verified and updated by an expert committee so that it aligns with global and national guidelines. For places with limited expert access, this is invaluable; even in expert settings, it ensures nothing is overlooked. Over time, as new evidence emerges (e.g., a new drug approved or a new resistance mechanism identified), the system’s knowledge base can be updated centrally and all users benefit immediately – a big advantage over static guidelines or retraining cycles. Importantly, this wouldn’t replace the clinician’s judgment or the formal approval processes (e.g., many countries have an MDR-TB committee that must approve regimens), but it would augment them, potentially making those committees faster or more standardized.
C. Integration with Existing Systems via APIs: To avoid data duplication and competition, the new solution should be built with interoperability in mind. For instance, it could offer a Nikshay connector (or generic connectors) that periodically fetches necessary data (with permission) – such as the line list of DR-TB patients and their DST results – to update the analytics. Similarly, an interface for the US context could connect to CDC or state databases for surveillance data (perhaps through a secure data exchange). If direct integration is not initially possible, allowing CSV or Excel import/export from common systems will help (most programs can export their data). This way, the tool sits on top of current workflows. For example, an Indian TB program manager could export a Nikshay DR-TB report and import it into the Resistance Hub to see advanced analytics that Nikshay’s dashboard might not provide. In the US, a state TB controller could import their last 5 years of cases to visualize trends in pyrazinamide resistance or to identify any pattern in genotype clusters versus treatment outcomes. By being interoperable, the solution avoids being seen as a silo; instead it positions itself as a value-adding layer on existing infrastructure.
D. Global Data Collaboration Features: Build in functionality that encourages sharing and comparing data in a governed way. For example, the platform could allow a country or state to opt-in to share anonymized aggregated indicators to a global view. This could result in a world map of, say, MDR-TB outcome success rates or prevalence of XDR-TB by country, updated more frequently than WHO’s annual report. It could also facilitate research collaboration: users (with the right permissions) could query pooled datasets. Another idea is a module for clinical trial support – e.g., if a new regimen is being trialed, the platform can help identify eligible patients or track outcomes under program conditions. By focusing on antibiotic resistance and outcomes, this would not conflict with patient management tools, but rather supply them and the research community with richer context. In essence, the platform becomes a bridge between programs, laboratories, and researchers.
E. Patient and Provider Engagement (Selective): While the emphasis is on data and decision support, some features could directly help providers and patients in ways not currently done. For example, a patient app extension could be offered specifically for DR-TB patients that ties into the system: it might have treatment reminders tailored to the complex regimens (many pills, longer duration), symptom tracking (patients can log side effects daily), and a channel to alert providers of any issues. The data from this could feed back to the provider interface – giving a more granular adherence and pharmacovigilance view than just whether a dose was taken. Many existing adherence tools (99DOTS, vDOT) handle the “taken or not” aspect; this DR-TB patient app could handle quality of daily life and side effects, which are crucial in MDR treatment. It could also deliver educational content specific to MDR-TB (which drugs and their side effects, importance of completing therapy even after culture conversion, etc.). For providers, the platform could include a contact forum or chat where they can discuss cases (perhaps an extension of the TB Consilium idea, but more peer-to-peer in real time). This builds a community of practice, complementing the formal expert advice with a more dynamic support network. Such features would have to be optional and carefully moderated, but they could increase engagement with the platform beyond just being a passive data tool.
F. Emphasis on Antibiotic Stewardship and New Knowledge: The solution should explicitly focus on antibiotic resistance not just by tracking it, but by helping reduce it. This includes not only ensuring proper treatment but also collecting data on why resistance happens. For example, it could incorporate a module to analyze treatment interruptions or failures and correlate them with resistance patterns – providing feedback to programs about potential system issues (like frequent stockouts or patient support failures leading to resistance). It could also integrate with genomic data to quickly flag emerging resistance mutations (if, say, a new mutation is found that causes bedaquiline resistance, the system could alert all users and update the decision support to avoid regimens relying solely on that drug for patients with that mutation). G. Modular and Scalable Design: Given different contexts, the platform should be modular. For instance, a country with robust systems might only use the advanced analytics module, whereas another with less developed systems might use more of its features (or even as a primary case management tool if they choose, though that’s not the target use-case). The decision support could be a standalone module that could integrate into other EMRs (for example, integrate via API into Nikshay or an EHR used in a US hospital TB clinic). Scalability and performance will be important because we are talking about large datasets (potentially millions of cases if multiple countries’ data is analyzed). Cloud-based deployment with strong data security would be ideal, given the sensitivity of TB patient data. Also, compliance with data protection laws (like India’s and HIPAA in the US) must be built-in from design.
